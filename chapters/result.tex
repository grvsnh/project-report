\chapter{RESULT}\label{ch:result}

\section{Demonstration Setup}\label{sec:demonstration}
The results in this chapter are based on the demonstrated Android prototype. The available
evidence consists of application screenshots in indoor settings, showing everyday objects and
groups of people. The screenshots display a confidence threshold of 0.25 and an IoU threshold of
0.70, together with task controls and runtime readings.

These exhibits document the prototype's visible behavior. The available material does not identify
the device model, annotated test-set size, model input resolution, execution backend, or timing
sample duration. Consequently, the reported values are transcriptions of displayed readings, and
the visual demonstrations are not presented as a controlled accuracy benchmark.

\subsection{Observed Configuration and Evaluation Boundary}
The camera preview, selected task mode, confidence and IoU thresholds, overlay type, and displayed
runtime fields form the minimum context needed to understand each demonstration, and recording them
with every run separates a change in model behavior from a change in interface settings.

The evidence also separates interface from model evidence. A live preview with an overlay confirms
that capture, inference, coordinate mapping, and rendering were connected for that frame, and the
class name and confidence value show what the model displayed, but neither proves the class correct;
a smooth preview suggests responsiveness during the capture without establishing a sustained frame
rate. The result is therefore a record of what the prototype visibly produced under the available
demonstration conditions, not a claim that it has been trained on a combat dataset, validated
against field imagery, or qualified for operational deployment. It remains useful because it
identifies the working capture-to-display path and makes the next evaluation steps concrete.

\subsection{Cross-Orientation and Task Comparison}
The exhibits cover both orientations: portrait scenes make the vertical camera workflow and the
stacked controls easy to inspect, while landscape scenes show the same output path when the available
horizontal space changes. This matters for a wearable or helmet-mounted concept, where orientation
changes as the operator moves.

Detection mode is the clearest first check, since its output is boxes, labels, and confidence values.
Segmentation adds a denser visual layer and tests whether masks stay aligned without obscuring the
scene, and pose adds keypoints and connections for multi-person rendering and partial occlusion.
The prototype presents all three through one interface, but the screenshots do not rank them by
accuracy or field suitability. The displayed scores also show why visual interpretation must
accompany numerical readings: a high score can make an overlay convincing while a lower score can
still be useful, and a plausible box can still be a false detection until compared with ground truth.

The landscape segmentation screenshot decomposes its 41.3 ms reading into 5.1 ms preprocessing,
32.6 ms inference, and 3.6 ms post-processing. The group-pose screenshot similarly shows 10.2 ms,
25.2 ms, and 2.5 ms, totaling 37.9 ms. In both snapshots, inference is the largest displayed
component, making model execution an important target for further profiling.

The displayed FPS is not simply the reciprocal of the processing-time field. For instance, 37.9 ms
corresponds to approximately 26.4 processing cycles per second, whereas the screenshot reports
21.3 FPS. Capture, scheduling, rendering, and the application's averaging conventions can affect
the relationship between these indicators. The timing windows are not defined, so the
processing-time field is not claimed to measure end-to-end camera-to-display latency.

The six readings are configuration-specific observations: the 11.9 FPS and 62.9 ms of portrait
segmentation is not comparable with the 21.3 FPS and 37.9 ms of landscape group pose because task,
scene, orientation, and processing path differ, and the component times identify where time was
displayed for those frames rather than a distribution over repeated samples. Inference is the visible
contributor to processing time, while displayed FPS also depends on capture, scheduling, rendering,
and averaging, so the table does not support a claim of stable real-time performance under all
conditions.

\subsection{Observation-Based Comparison}
The six displayed readings can still be compared descriptively when their context is retained. The
portrait segmentation exhibit has the lowest displayed FPS and the largest displayed processing time
in the table. This is consistent with segmentation presenting a denser output than a simple detection
box, but the available evidence does not isolate the task from the scene, device state, or application
averaging. It is therefore an observation about the supplied configuration, not a measured general
property of every segmentation run.

The landscape group-pose exhibit has the highest displayed FPS among the six readings, while the
portrait group-pose exhibit reports a lower displayed FPS. The difference shows why orientation should
be recorded, but it does not prove that landscape processing is intrinsically faster. The scenes may
contain different numbers of visible people, the preview dimensions may differ, and the timing field may
use a rolling or task-specific calculation. A controlled comparison would hold the device, scene,
model, resolution, and number of samples constant while changing only the orientation.

The visual exhibits provide a second form of comparison. Detection outputs are comparatively sparse
and easy to inspect, segmentation outputs cover regions of the image, and pose outputs add several
keypoints and connecting lines. As visual complexity increases, readability and overlay placement become
important interface concerns even when the underlying model call is unchanged. The screenshots support
this interface-level comparison, but they do not establish which task is more accurate or more suitable
for deployment.

\subsection{Recommended Evaluation Protocol}
The next evaluation should begin with a documented test set covering the intended operating
conditions: object classes, image sources, scene conditions, distance and scale ranges, lighting
variation, occlusion cases, and the annotation procedure, with the test split kept separate from any
training data and any combat-specific classes and annotation policy stated before scores are computed.

For accuracy, predictions should be matched against annotations under a declared IoU rule and one
confidence-processing policy across runs, with precision, recall, and mAP reported alongside the
dataset size and split rather than as a single percentage. Pose and segmentation require their own
task-specific metrics, and false positives and missed detections should be reported because an
aggregate can hide failures on small, distant, or partially occluded objects.

For performance, the device model, Android version, build, model export, input resolution, execution
delegate, battery condition, and thermal state should be recorded, with each task and orientation run
through a warm-up period followed by a fixed measurement interval and the samples reported with mean
and spread. Camera-to-display latency should be measured separately from model processing time, and
power and thermal figures should come from a documented instrument or system method.

The procedure should include normal and adverse conditions: stable overlays and predictable controls on
one side, motion, glare, low light, clutter, occlusion, changing distance, and orientation transitions
on the other, recording whether the application recovers after a permission interruption, a dropped
frame, or a temporary model error. Until this protocol is completed, the current results remain a
functional demonstration with configuration-specific runtime observations: a working capture,
inference, mapping, and rendering path, with a clear method for the next study.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
The demonstration establishes a functional foundation of live camera input, on-device inference,
task-specific visualization, configurable thresholds, and displayed runtime information, supporting
continued development of a portable situational-awareness application; however, its classes are
predominantly ordinary objects and people, not a validated combat-specific threat vocabulary.

Quantitative accuracy evaluation requires an annotated dataset and defined test split; precision, recall,
and mAP are not reported because the available material lacks those measurements. Sustained FPS,
latency distributions, power consumption, and thermal behavior likewise require repeated runs on a
documented device, and literature-review results belong to those studies, not to VeerDrishti.

The results can consequently be organized into three evidence levels. Directly observed evidence
includes camera preview operation, object overlays, segmentation regions, pose keypoints, threshold
controls, and displayed runtime fields; implementation-supported behavior is the documented capture,
rotation, preprocessing, inference, and rendering sequence; and claims still requiring new evidence are
combat-specific recognition, accuracy against annotated data, sustained performance, power and thermal
behavior, tracking consistency, and operational reliability. The classification keeps a prototype
screenshot from being presented as a field evaluation.

Further evaluation should cover small or distant objects, occlusion, camera motion, illumination changes,
false or missed detections, and overlay alignment, while combat-specific fine-tuning, thermal fusion,
open-vocabulary recognition, and persistent event storage remain future work.
