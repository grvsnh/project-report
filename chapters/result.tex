\chapter{RESULT}\label{ch:result}

\section{Demonstration Setup}\label{sec:demonstration}
The results in this chapter are based on the demonstrated Android prototype. The available
evidence consists of application screenshots in indoor settings, showing everyday objects and
groups of people. The screenshots display a confidence threshold of 0.25 and an IoU threshold of
0.70, together with task controls and runtime readings.

These exhibits document the prototype's visible behavior. The available material does not identify
the device model, annotated test-set size, model input resolution, execution backend, or timing
sample duration. Consequently, the reported values are transcriptions of displayed readings, and
the visual demonstrations are not presented as a controlled accuracy benchmark.

\subsection{Observed Configuration and Evaluation Boundary}
The visible prototype configuration provides a useful functional baseline for interpreting the
remaining results. The camera preview, selected task mode, confidence threshold, IoU threshold,
overlay type, and displayed runtime fields form the minimum context needed to understand each
demonstration. Recording these values with every future run will make comparisons between scenes
more reliable and will distinguish a change in model behavior from a change in interface settings.

The current evidence also separates what can be observed directly from what still requires testing.
An overlay confirms that a frame passed through capture, inference, coordinate mapping, and display;
it does not confirm class accuracy, stable tracking, or suitability for operational deployment. A
proper evaluation should therefore pair the screenshots with the device configuration, model file,
input resolution, dataset split, repeated timing samples, and clearly defined success criteria.

\section{Displayed Runtime Measurements}\label{sec:runtime}
The frame-rate and processing-time readings visible in the six exhibits are recorded in
Table~\ref{tab:runtime}. The values span 11.9--21.3 FPS and 30.8--62.9 ms of displayed processing time. They
refer to different scenes and task modes, so they should not be treated as an average over one
uniform experiment or used to rank model variants without additional test controls.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Displayed Runtime Readings}\label{tab:runtime}
  \begin{tabular}{p{7.3cm}crr}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Exhibit} & \textbf{View} & \textbf{FPS} & \textbf{Time (ms)} \\
    \midrule
    Exhibit A: Portrait scissors detection & Portrait & 19.5 & 35.6 \\
    Exhibit B: Portrait segmentation & Portrait & 11.9 & 62.9 \\
    Exhibit C: Portrait group pose & Portrait & 19.1 & 30.8 \\
    Exhibit D: Landscape mouse and scissors & Landscape & 16.3 & 54.2 \\
    Exhibit E: Landscape interior segmentation & Landscape & 16.0 & 41.3 \\
    Exhibit F: Landscape group pose & Landscape & 21.3 & 37.9 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The landscape segmentation screenshot decomposes its 41.3 ms reading into 5.1 ms preprocessing,
32.6 ms inference, and 3.6 ms post-processing. The group-pose screenshot similarly shows 10.2 ms,
25.2 ms, and 2.5 ms, totaling 37.9 ms. In both snapshots, inference is the largest displayed
component, making model execution an important target for further profiling.

The displayed FPS is not simply the reciprocal of the processing-time field. For instance, 37.9 ms
corresponds to approximately 26.4 processing cycles per second, whereas the screenshot reports
21.3 FPS. Capture, scheduling, rendering, and the application's averaging conventions can affect
the relationship between these indicators. The timing windows are not defined, so the
processing-time field is not claimed to measure end-to-end camera-to-display latency.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
The demonstration establishes a functional foundation of live camera input, on-device inference,
task-specific visualization, configurable thresholds, and displayed runtime information, supporting
continued development of a portable situational-awareness application; however, its classes are
predominantly ordinary objects and people, not a validated combat-specific threat vocabulary.

Quantitative accuracy evaluation requires an annotated dataset and defined test split; precision, recall,
and mAP are not reported because the available material lacks those measurements. Sustained FPS,
latency distributions, power consumption, and thermal behavior likewise require repeated runs on a
documented device. Literature-review results belong to those studies, not to VeerDrishti.

Further evaluation should cover small or distant objects, occlusion, camera motion, illumination changes,
false or missed detections, and overlay alignment. Combat-specific fine-tuning, thermal fusion,
open-vocabulary recognition, and persistent event storage remain future work requiring separate
implementation, evaluation, and controlled robustness testing.
